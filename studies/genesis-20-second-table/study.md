# The Second Table in Gerar

**A five-session Reformed study of commandments six through ten in
Genesis 20, anchored in Hebrews.**

Scripture quotations follow the ESV. Catechism references use the
Westminster Shorter (WSC), Westminster Larger (WLC), and Heidelberg (HC)
catechisms. Numbering of the commandments is the Reformed order — sixth
murder, seventh adultery, eighth theft, ninth false witness, tenth
coveting. (Lutheran and Roman numbering divides coveting into two and
joins the first and second; this study uses the Westminster/Heidelberg
order throughout.)

**Format:** five sessions, 45–60 minutes each, one commandment per week.
Read all eighteen verses of Genesis 20 aloud at the start of *every*
session — it is short, and the point of the study is that one episode
carries all five commandments at once.

---

## Opening frame — before session one

### The two tables

The Ten Words divide. The first four govern love of God; the last six
govern love of neighbor. Jesus makes the division explicit in Matthew
22:37–40, and Paul gathers the second table into one sentence: "Love
does no wrong to a neighbor; therefore love is the fulfilling of the
law" (Romans 13:9–10). Commandments six through ten are the second
table — the load-bearing wall between a person and everyone else.

### Four Reformed rules for reading them

The Larger Catechism (Q. 99) gives rules for understanding the
commandments. Four of them govern this study:

1. **The law is spiritual.** It binds the understanding, will,
   affections, and everything else — not merely outward behavior.
2. **A negative implies a positive.** Where a sin is forbidden, the
   contrary duty is commanded; where a duty is commanded, the contrary
   sin is forbidden.
3. **One sin names its whole family.** Under one sin or duty, all of the
   same kind are included — with their causes, means, occasions, and
   appearances.
4. **We are our neighbor's keeper.** We are bound to do what we can that
   others keep the commandment too, according to our place.

Rule 3 is why Genesis 20 is not five separate sins loosely stacked. It
is one act of unbelief branching into five.

### The three uses of the law

- **First (civil):** restrains sin in society, believer and unbeliever
  alike. Watch for it in Abimelech.
- **Second (pedagogical):** a mirror. It shows sin, kills self-
  confidence, and drives us to Christ. Watch for it in Abraham.
- **Third (didactic):** the rule of grateful obedience for the redeemed.
  This is why Heidelberg places the commandments in Part Three —
  *Gratitude* (Q&A 92–115), after the sections on sin and deliverance,
  not before. The law does not save us; it shows the saved how to live.

### The setting

Genesis 20 falls late. Abraham has already been justified by faith
(Genesis 15:6; Romans 4:3). He has already received circumcision as the
sign of the covenant (Genesis 17). He has just interceded for Sodom
(Genesis 18). The promise of a son is one chapter away — Isaac is born
in Genesis 21:1–2.

And here, in that late season, Abraham repeats verbatim the sin of
Genesis 12:10–20. He is not a young man learning. He is a mature
believer running an old, settled policy (20:13).

**The question the whole study turns on:** if the friend of God can do
this after all that, what is the law actually for?

## Session VI — "You shall not murder"

> **The law.** "You shall not murder." — Exodus 20:13; Deuteronomy 5:17

### In Gerar

Read Genesis 20:3, 20:7, 20:11, 20:18.

The chapter is soaked in death. God's first word to Abimelech is a death
sentence: "Behold, you are a dead man because of the woman whom you have
taken, for she is a man's wife" (20:3). The sentence extends to the whole
household — "you shall surely die, you and all who are yours" (20:7).
And there is a quieter death running underneath: "the LORD had closed all
the wombs of the house of Abimelech" (20:18). A kingdom with no future.

Abraham's own explanation is the hinge: "I did it because I thought,
There is no fear of God at all in this place, and they will kill me
because of my wife" (20:11).

Notice what he does with that fear. He does not flee, pray, or tell the
truth and trust God with the outcome. He arranges for his wife to absorb
the danger instead of him. He preserves his own life by spending hers.

### The Reformed lens

WSC 68 — the sixth commandment *requires* "all lawful endeavours to
preserve our own life, and the life of others." WSC 69 forbids taking
life "unjustly, or whatsoever tendeth thereunto." That last clause is
the indictment: Abraham never lifts a hand, and he still stands on the
wrong side of the sixth commandment, because he set in motion what tends
toward another's death.

WLC 136 spells out the same reach — anything that tends to the taking
away of life. Heidelberg 105–107 pushes it to the root: God forbids not
only killing but envy, hatred, anger, and the desire for revenge, and
positively requires that I love my neighbor, be patient with them, and
do good even to my enemies.

**Note the reversal.** Abraham assumes Gerar has no fear of God. Gerar
turns out to be full of it: "the men were very much afraid" (20:8).
Abraham's assessment of the pagans was itself a form of contempt — and
contempt is where the sixth commandment starts.

### The Hebrews anchor

> "Since therefore the children share in flesh and blood, he himself
> likewise partook of the same things, that through death he might
> destroy the one who has the power of death, that is, the devil, and
> deliver all those who through fear of death were subject to lifelong
> slavery." — Hebrews 2:14–15

Fear of death is not a footnote in Genesis 20. It is the engine. Abraham
is a believer in lifelong slavery to a fear Christ came to break. Pair it
with Hebrews 13:6 — "The Lord is my helper; I will not fear; what can man
do to me?" — which is the sentence Abraham could not say in Gerar.

### Questions

1. Abraham never touches a weapon. On what grounds does the sixth
   commandment still accuse him?
2. Where does self-preservation become a sin against a neighbor rather
   than a duty toward yourself (WSC 68 requires both)?
3. Whose safety do you quietly assume will absorb the cost of your
   fear — a spouse, a child, an employee, a friend?
4. What would Abraham have had to believe about God at verse 11 in order
   to tell the truth at verse 2?

### Confession and prayer

Confess fear of man as a form of unbelief, not merely a weakness. Pray
Hebrews 13:6 back as a petition, not a slogan.

## Session VII — "You shall not commit adultery"

> **The law.** "You shall not commit adultery." — Exodus 20:14;
> Deuteronomy 5:18

### In Gerar

Read Genesis 20:2–6 and 20:16.

"And Abimelech king of Gerar sent and took Sarah" (20:2). God's
objection is stated as a bare fact of created order: "she is a man's
wife" (20:3). Not "she is under a contract" — a bond God recognizes and
defends whether or not the parties disclose it.

Then the sentence that reorganizes the whole chapter: "it was I who kept
you from sinning against me. Therefore I did not let you touch her"
(20:6).

Sarah's chastity is not preserved by her husband. It is not preserved by
her own resourcefulness. It is preserved by God restraining a pagan king
from a sin the king did not know he was committing.

### The Reformed lens

WSC 71 — the seventh commandment requires "the preservation of our own
and our neighbour's chastity, in heart, speech, and behaviour." WSC 72
forbids all unchaste thoughts, words, and actions. Heidelberg 108–109
adds that God condemns not only the act but everything that incites it,
and calls body and soul together a temple of the Holy Spirit.

Now apply rule 4 from the opening frame — we are bound to help others
keep the commandment. Abraham was Sarah's covenant head. His assigned
work under the seventh commandment was to guard her. He organized her
exposure instead, and God had to do the guarding.

**Restraining grace.** Abimelech's "integrity of heart and innocence of
hands" (20:5) is real at the level of his intent — and God says plainly
it was *God's* doing, not the king's virtue (20:6). This is common grace:
God restraining sin in people outside the covenant, here specifically to
protect the line that Isaac will come from. Abimelech's clean hands are
a gift, not an achievement. So are most of ours.

**Sarah is publicly vindicated** (20:16) — a thousand pieces of silver,
"a sign of your innocence in the eyes of all who are with you." God does
not leave the wronged party's reputation in the wreckage of the wrong.

### The Hebrews anchor

> "Let marriage be held in honor among all, and let the marriage bed be
> undefiled, for God will judge the sexually immoral and adulterous."
> — Hebrews 13:4

Read it against 20:2–6 and the irony lands hard. The one who held the
marriage in *dishonor* was the husband. The pagan court had to be warned
off by direct revelation. Hebrews says God will judge — Genesis 20 shows
Him doing exactly that, in a dream, before dawn.

### Questions

1. God calls Sarah "a man's wife" before either man in the story treats
   her that way. What does that say about who defines marriage?
2. Verse 6 says God kept Abimelech from sinning. Where has God restrained
   you from a sin you were fully prepared to commit — and did you notice
   at the time?
3. WSC 71 requires preserving our *neighbour's* chastity, not only our
   own. What does that duty look like concretely in your household, and
   in what you publish, share, and watch?
4. Abraham's failure was not lust. It was refusing to spend himself to
   protect his wife. Is that a seventh-commandment sin? Argue it from
   WSC 71.

### Confession and prayer

Thank God for restraints you did not ask for. Confess the protective
duties you have quietly outsourced to God, or to no one.

## Session VIII — "You shall not steal"

> **The law.** "You shall not steal." — Exodus 20:15; Deuteronomy 5:19

### In Gerar

Read Genesis 20:2, 20:7, 20:14–16.

The verb in verse 2 is *took*. What was taken was not livestock but a
person — a wife lifted out of a marriage. God's remedy is the language of
returned property: "return the man's wife" (20:7).

Then the restitution. Abimelech gives sheep, oxen, male and female
servants, a thousand pieces of silver, and the run of his land: "Behold,
my land is before you; dwell where it pleases you" (20:15). This is far
beyond what Exodus 22 would later require. The pagan king over-restores.

And Abraham keeps it. He leaves Gerar richer than he entered — exactly
as he left Egypt richer in Genesis 12:16. Twice now, the lie has paid.

### The Reformed lens

WSC 74 — the eighth commandment requires "the lawful procuring and
furthering the wealth and outward estate of ourselves and others." WSC 75
forbids "whatsoever doth or may unjustly hinder our own or our
neighbour's wealth or outward estate." WLC 141 names restitution, truth
and faithfulness in contracts, and giving and lending freely as positive
duties; WLC 142 lists fraud, oppression, and unlawful callings among what
is forbidden.

Hold Abraham against that. He *hindered* Abimelech's estate — the wombs
of the household were closed on account of what Abraham set up (20:18) —
and then received payment for the damage he caused. The gain is real; the
title to it is not.

This is the session that will make the room uncomfortable, and it should.
The eighth commandment is not only about how money is *taken*. It is
about how money is *kept*. Repentance has a ledger.

### The Hebrews anchor

> "Keep your life free from love of money, and be content with what you
> have, for he has said, 'I will never leave you nor forsake you.'"
> — Hebrews 13:5

The security Abraham tried to manufacture by a lie is the security
Hebrews says he already had by promise. Pair it with Hebrews 10:34, where
believers "joyfully accepted the plundering of your property, since you
knew that you yourselves had a better possession and an abiding one."

That is the whole difference. A believer who has a better and abiding
possession can afford to lose. Abraham, in Gerar, could not afford to
lose — so he took.

### Questions

1. Is a person property? On what grounds does the eighth commandment
   apply to Genesis 20:2 at all?
2. Abimelech restores more than he took. Abraham restores nothing. What
   does the narrator accomplish by putting those two in the same chapter?
3. WSC 74 makes *furthering your neighbour's* estate a positive duty of
   the eighth commandment. Name one concrete way you could obey that this
   month.
4. Is there money, standing, or advantage in your life that arrived
   through something you would not want examined? What does repentance
   actually require you to do with it?

### Confession and prayer

Pray Hebrews 13:5 as a confession of contentment, naming one specific
thing you are gripping.

## Session IX — "You shall not bear false witness"

> **The law.** "You shall not bear false witness against your neighbor."
> — Exodus 20:16; Deuteronomy 5:20

### In Gerar

Read Genesis 20:2, 20:5, 20:9–13.

"He said of Sarah his wife, 'She is my sister'" (20:2).

Then the defense, when he is caught: "Besides, she is indeed my sister,
the daughter of my father though not the daughter of my mother, and she
became my wife" (20:12).

It is true. Every word of it is technically true. And it was engineered
to produce a false belief in the hearer — which it did, all the way to a
king's household and a closed womb.

Then the worst verse in the chapter: "And when God caused me to wander
from my father's house, I said to her, 'This is the kindness you must do
me: at every place to which we come, say of me, He is my brother'"
(20:13). This was not panic. It was **standing policy**. Abraham
institutionalized his unbelief and called it kindness.

And Abimelech — the man with no covenant, no promise, no revelation until
last night — delivers the rebuke: "You have done to me things that ought
not to be done" (20:9).

### The Reformed lens

WSC 77 — the ninth commandment requires "the maintaining and promoting of
truth between man and man, and of our own and our neighbour's good name,
especially in witness-bearing." WSC 78 forbids whatever is prejudicial to
truth or injurious to a good name.

The Larger Catechism is startlingly precise here. Among the sins
forbidden by the ninth commandment, WLC 145 explicitly names
**equivocation and mental reservation** — saying something true in a
sense you know your hearer will not take. That is Genesis 20:12, defined
in advance by the catechism. Heidelberg 112 says the same from the other
side: I must not twist anyone's words, must love the truth, speak it
candidly, and defend my neighbor's honor.

**The deeper false witness.** Abraham's premise in 20:11 — "there is no
fear of God at all in this place" — was a claim about God's reach. He
testified, by how he behaved, that God's protection stopped at the border
of Gerar. That was the lie underneath the lie. Every deception is
finally a statement about what we believe God will and will not do.

### The Hebrews anchor

> "So that by two unchangeable things, in which it is impossible for God
> to lie, we who have fled for refuge might have strong encouragement to
> hold fast to the hope set before us. We have this as a sure and
> steadfast anchor of the soul." — Hebrews 6:18–19

Hebrews 6 is *about Abraham* — God's oath to Abraham is the case study
(6:13–15). The chapter's whole argument is that God secured the promise
with an oath because He cannot lie. Set that beside a man who cannot stop
lying about the very promise he carries.

Then read Hebrews 4:12–13: "no creature is hidden from his sight, but all
are naked and exposed to the eyes of him to whom we must give account."
Genesis 20:3 is that verse in narrative form. The scheme lasted until God
decided to speak, and not one minute longer.

### Questions

1. Is a true statement designed to deceive a lie? Answer from WLC 145
   before answering from instinct.
2. Abraham's arrangement in 20:13 was made years in advance. What
   deceptions have you *systematized* rather than committed in a moment?
3. What did Abraham's behavior testify about God to the people of Gerar?
   What does yours testify to the people who watch you work?
4. Why does the outsider get the moral high ground in verse 9, and what
   is the narrator doing to the reader by arranging it that way?

### Confession and prayer

Confess a specific half-truth by name, not deception in general. Read
Hebrews 6:18 aloud as the counter-testimony.

## Session X — "You shall not covet"

> **The law.** "You shall not covet your neighbor's house; you shall not
> covet your neighbor's wife, or his male servant, or his female servant,
> or his ox, or his donkey, or anything that is your neighbor's."
> — Exodus 20:17; Deuteronomy 5:21

### In Gerar

Read Genesis 20:2, 20:11, 20:13 again — and this time read them for
*desire*, not action.

The tenth commandment names "your neighbor's wife" out loud. Abimelech's
taking in verse 2 began before verse 2, in wanting. But the sharper
reading is the one that indicts the believer: Abraham's covetousness is
for his own survival, his own comfort, his own standing — and Sarah is
the instrument he is willing to spend to get it.

That is what coveting does. It converts a person into a means.

The tenth commandment is the one that cannot be kept outwardly, because
it has no outward form. It is the commandment that proves the other nine
were never merely about behavior. Paul says exactly this: "I would not
have known what it is to covet if the law had not said, 'You shall not
covet'" (Romans 7:7).

### The Reformed lens

WSC 80 — the tenth commandment requires "full contentment with our own
condition, with a right and charitable frame of spirit toward our
neighbour, and all that is his." WSC 81 forbids "all discontentment with
our own estate, envying or grieving at the good of our neighbour, and all
inordinate motions and affections to any thing that is his."

*Inordinate motions and affections.* Not acts. Motions. The law reaches
into the part of you that no one audits.

This is where Heidelberg drives the study home:

- **HC 114** — Can those converted to God keep these commandments
  perfectly? No. Even the holiest have only a small beginning of this
  obedience in this life.
- **HC 115** — Then why does God have them preached so strictly? So that
  all our life long we come to know our sinful nature more and more, and
  so that we press on toward perfection, praying for the grace of the
  Holy Spirit.

Genesis 20 is HC 114 in narrative. Abraham is genuinely converted, and he
has a small beginning of obedience — visible in 20:17, where he prays for
the man he wronged. The chapter refuses both the lie that believers
outgrow sin and the lie that sin proves you were never a believer.

### The Hebrews anchor

> "These all died in faith, not having received the things promised, but
> having seen them and greeted them from afar... they desired a better
> country, that is, a heavenly one." — Hebrews 11:13, 16

The cure for coveting is not less desire. It is desire aimed higher.
Hebrews 11:10 says Abraham "was looking forward to the city that has
foundations, whose designer and builder is God." In Gerar he was looking
forward to getting out of town alive. Same man, same appetite, aimed two
different directions.

Pair it with Hebrews 13:5 again — contentment grounded not in having
enough but in "I will never leave you nor forsake you."

### Questions

1. Why does the tenth commandment have to exist if the first nine are
   already given?
2. Where does Abraham treat Sarah as a means rather than a person, and
   what desire is he serving when he does it?
3. HC 114 says even the holiest have only a small beginning of obedience.
   How does that keep you from despair *and* from complacency at the same
   time?
4. Name the desire under your most persistent sin. Where would that same
   desire go if it were aimed at "a better country"?

### Confession and prayer

Pray HC 115 as a request: that God would use the strictness of the law to
show you yourself, and then give the Spirit to press on.

## Closing — the better word

Read Genesis 20:17–18, then Genesis 21:1–2.

Two things end the episode, and they should end the study.

**First, Abraham prays.** "Then Abraham prayed to God, and God healed
Abimelech, and also healed his wife and female slaves so that they bore
children" (20:17). The offender intercedes for the offended, at God's own
instruction (20:7, "he is a prophet, so that he will pray for you"), and
the wombs open.

It is a shadow — a thin one — of something better:

> "Consequently, he is able to save to the uttermost those who draw near
> to God through him, since he always lives to make intercession for
> them." — Hebrews 7:25

Abraham interceded once, for one household, having caused the damage
himself. Christ intercedes always, for all who come, having caused none
of it.

**Second, God keeps the promise anyway.** "The LORD visited Sarah as he
had said, and the LORD did to Sarah as he had promised. And Sarah
conceived and bore Abraham a son in his old age at the time of which God
had spoken to him" (21:1–2).

The man carrying the promise nearly bartered it away in Gerar. Isaac
arrives on schedule. Not because Abraham finally cleared the second
table — the next chapter proves nothing about his performance — but
because the promise never rested on him.

And when Hebrews 11 lists Abraham's faith, Gerar is not in the record.
Not because it did not happen, but because it was covered.

> "...and to Jesus, the mediator of a new covenant, and to the sprinkled
> blood that speaks a better word than the blood of Abel." — Hebrews 12:24

The second table accuses. Abel's blood cried out from the ground for
justice. The sprinkled blood speaks a better word over the same
evidence — and it is speaking it over Genesis 20.

Close with Hebrews 12:1–2: lay aside the weight, run with endurance,
looking to Jesus, the founder and perfecter of our faith.
