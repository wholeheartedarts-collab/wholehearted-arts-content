## Hook

89% of small businesses now use AI in some form.

Almost none of them use it to create. They use it to stop drowning in the busywork around the thing they actually do.

## Body

That distinction matters more than people think, and it's the same one I live by in my own studio.

I don't let AI paint. The ideas, the color decisions, the hours at the easel — that's mine, and it stays mine. But the invoicing, the scheduling, the endless small admin tasks that have nothing to do with the actual work? That's exactly where automation earns its keep.

It's a pattern I've seen outside my studio too. Textile artists in workshops across India are feeding fabric patterns and traditional dyeing techniques into AI systems — not to replace the craft, but to turn heritage technique into digital prototypes they can weave from. The AI translates. The artist still decides what's worth making.

Small businesses using AI this way are reporting real results: over half save more than 20 hours a month, with average savings around $7,500 a year. Not because a machine got creative — because a machine got the paperwork off their desk.

That's the gap I kept noticing as an artist trying to build a second career: most AI tools are built for generic "business efficiency." Almost none of them are built by someone who actually understands what it's like to run a one-person creative operation — where every hour spent on scheduling is an hour not spent making anything.

So I started building automation for exactly that kind of business. Mine first. Now others.
