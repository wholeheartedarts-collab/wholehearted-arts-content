## Executive summary

"Wisdom of Mothers" draws on Proverbs 31:10-31, the Hebrew Bible's
acrostic poem praising a woman of noble character — often called "the
Proverbs 31 woman" in contemporary Christian teaching. The passage is
frequently cited in sermons, devotionals, and Christian art around
Mother's Day and women's ministry contexts, and its closing verses
explicitly ground the woman's praise in reverence for God rather than
outward beauty ("charm is deceptive, and beauty is fleeting; but a woman
who fears the LORD is to be praised" — Proverbs 31:30, NIV). This gives
the painting a well-documented, doctrinally mainstream scriptural anchor
well suited to the client's faith/family/praising-God content pillars.

## Key points

- Proverbs 31:10-31 is structured as an acrostic poem — each of its 22
  verses begins with a successive letter of the Hebrew alphabet, a
  literary device signaling completeness/totality (source: Christianity.com,
  Bible Study Tools).
- The passage catalogs the woman's industriousness (trading, textile
  work, managing a household, providing for the poor) alongside her
  character (wisdom, strength, dignity), not physical beauty alone.
- Verse 30 is the passage's own stated thesis: enduring worth comes from
  "fear of the LORD" (reverence for God), not charm or beauty — directly
  supports a faith-rooted (not merely inspirational) framing.
- "Her children arise and call her blessed; her husband also, and he
  praises her" (v. 28) — the passage explicitly frames the woman's legacy
  as multi-generational, which lines up with the catalog description's
  "carried by women across generations" language.
- Contemporary Christian commentary (CBE International) reframes the
  passage's "strength" (Hebrew: chayil, also used of warriors) as
  strength exercised in service to others, not domination — useful
  framing if a caption wants to avoid a purely domestic/traditionalist
  reading and lean into "quiet strength" as the catalog description
  itself already phrases it.

  Christian circles — some writers (see Esther Press source below) argue
  the passage is often misapplied as a rigid domestic checklist/goal
