## Executive summary

"Children — Reward from the LORD" is a 20"x20" framed mixed-media (fabric,
twine, acrylic) Studio Collection piece depicting a father and mother
praying over their unborn child, with Psalm 127:1-3 (ESV) written directly
into the canvas in white pen. The content angle is children as a gift from
God rather than a human achievement — fitting squarely into the client's
faith/prayer and family content pillars, and framed around parents' shared
prayer rather than any one parent's experience.

## Risks, counterarguments, or limitations

- Risk of implying the post speaks to every family's experience of
  childbearing (including loss, infertility, or waiting) — mitigated by
  keeping the post specific to this painting and this prayer, not making
  a universal claim about parenthood.
- Psalm 127:3's "children are a heritage... a reward" could, read outside
  context, sound transactional; the post should keep the emphasis on the
  passage's actual point (dependence on God, not human effort or merit)
  rather than reducing children to a "reward" for righteousness.
- Not a political or cultural-commentary topic; no politics risk beyond
  general care with how the scripture is framed.

## Possible content angles

1. **Children as gift, not achievement** — Psalm 127's full arc (futile
   self-reliant striving → God-given rest → children as God's gift), read
   through the painting's depiction of parents praying together. (Chosen
   angle — uses the whole passage, not just v.3 in isolation.)
2. The physical materials (fabric, twine) as a metaphor for how a family is
   woven/held together (process/material-focused).
3. A direct, standalone reflection on prayer for an unborn child
   specifically (narrower — risks sounding like it addresses only
   expecting parents, a smaller slice of the audience than the painting's
   broader "children are a gift" message).
4. The futility verses (v.1-2) alone, applied to burnout/anxious striving
   more broadly, independent of children (rejected — underuses the
   painting's actual depicted subject).
