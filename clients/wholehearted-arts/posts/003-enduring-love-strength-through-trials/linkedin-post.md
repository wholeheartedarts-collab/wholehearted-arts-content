## Hook

Most people get married hoping to be happy.

The couples still married 40, 50 years later? At some point they stopped chasing that — and started building something instead.

## Body

I made a piece last year called "Enduring Love; Strength through Trials," and I keep coming back to look at it myself, which doesn't happen often.

I started it not long after hearing about a young couple who never got the chance to grow old together. I couldn't stop thinking about the fifty years they wouldn't get to have.

So I painted an elderly Jewish couple instead — wrapped in each other, faces weathered by every year they had lived through. Behind them, Jerusalem's silhouette — the temple, the skyline, a city that's been torn down and rebuilt more times than anyone can count.

That's the whole idea of the painting. The couple and the city mirror each other. Both have been broken open by hard seasons. Both are still standing — not untouched, but renewed.

I don't think that happens by accident. And I don't think it happens because two people simply stayed happy with each other for half a century.

Ephesians puts it differently: "Husbands, love your wives, just as Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her."

That's not a formula for a good mood. That's a picture of two people building a union meant to say something to everyone watching — a love that stays, on purpose, through the parts that aren't easy.

I love watching couples who've done this. The ones who've been through the version of marriage nobody puts on a highlight reel and are still choosing each other on the other side of it.

Here's something that made me smile when I found out about it: in Poland, couples who reach 50 years of marriage are awarded the Medal za Długoletnie Pożycie Małżeńskie — the Medal for Long Marital Life, given in the name of the President of Poland. An entire country decided that lasting love deserves an actual honor. I wish we did something like that here.
